Emergency Radio Communications Guide

Ham (Amateur), CB, Shortwave, GMRS/FRS & MURS — how they work and how to use them

This guide explains, in plain language, how to communicate by radio when the internet and phones are down. It covers what each type of radio is, what it can do, which frequencies/channels matter in an emergency, and the basic procedures for calling for help.

Need actual channel numbers and frequencies? Open the offline, printable Radio Frequency Reference (Markdown). It includes weather, personal radio, marine, aviation, beacons and amateur band plans.

US legal note: Amateur and GMRS transmitting normally requires the applicable license. CB, FRS and MURS use license-by-rule with compliant equipment and service limits. Receiving public broadcasts generally needs no transmit license, but local scanner, interception and disclosure restrictions can apply. 47 CFR 97.403 and 97.405 concern amateur stations in distress/emergencies; they are not blanket permission for anyone to transmit on any frequency. Follow the rules of the service and country you are in.

Three things everyone should know:

  1. Monitor, don't just transmit. A cheap receiver tells you what's happening.
  2. Higher antenna = more range. Antenna height matters more than watts.

2. How radio actually works (the 60-second version)

  • A radio turns your voice into radio waves at a certain frequency (measured in kilohertz kHz or megahertz MHz). Another radio tuned to the same frequency turns the wave back into sound.
  • Both radios must be on the same frequency/channel AND the same mode (e.g. FM, AM, SSB) to hear each other.
  • Only one person talks at a time. You press PTT (push-to-talk) to transmit, release it to listen. You cannot hear others while you hold PTT.
  • Frequency bands behave very differently:
    • VHF/UHF (high frequency numbers, ~30 MHz–3 GHz): travel in roughly straight "line of sight." Great locally, blocked by hills/buildings. Used by FRS, GMRS, MURS, and ham 2m/70cm. Extended by repeaters (see below).
    • HF / shortwave (low numbers, ~3–30 MHz): can bounce off the ionosphere and travel hundreds or thousands of miles ("skip"). Used by ham HF, CB (27 MHz), and shortwave broadcasters. Range changes with time of day and the sun.

3. CB Radio (Citizens Band) — no license, simple, vehicle-friendly

  • What it is: 40 channels around 27 MHz (in the HF band). No license needed. Cheap, everywhere, popular with truckers and off-roaders.
  • Range: usually 1–15 miles (more in flat/open terrain; occasionally hundreds of miles when "skip" conditions bounce signals off the ionosphere).
  • Power limit: 4 watts AM / 12 watts SSB (SSB radios reach farther).
  • Key channels:
    • Channel 9 — traditional emergency / help channel.
    • Channel 19 — highway / trucker channel (road conditions, traffic, convoys).
    • Channel 1–40 — general use; pick an unused one for your group.
  • Modes: Most CB is AM. Higher-end radios add SSB (USB/LSB) for more range — but both radios must use the same mode.
  • Good for: vehicle-to-vehicle, convoys, short local coordination with zero setup.

4. FRS / GMRS / MURS — the walkie-talkie families

These are the little handheld radios ("walkie-talkies"). Great for family/group.

FRS (Family Radio Service) — no license

  • The blister-pack walkie-talkies. 22 channels in UHF (462/467 MHz).
  • Low power (0.5–2 W), fixed antenna. Range ~0.5–2 miles.
  • No license, no setup. Good for keeping a family/group in touch on foot.

GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) — cheap license, no test

  • Shares channels with FRS but allows higher power (up to 50 W), detachable antennas, and repeaters — so range can reach 5–50+ miles via a repeater.
  • One GMRS license (~$35, no exam) covers your entire family for 10 years.
  • Best all-round family emergency radio: cheap, legal, powerful, easy.

MURS (Multi-Use Radio Service) — no license

  • 5 channels in VHF (151/154 MHz), up to 2 W. No license.
  • VHF often works better outdoors/rural than FRS/GMRS UHF. Good for property/farm.

5. Ham (Amateur) Radio — the serious emergency option

Ham radio is the backbone of emergency communications. When everything else fails, hams relay health-and-welfare traffic, weather, and coordination across towns, states, and continents.

Licensing (USA) — easier than you think

Three license levels, each a multiple-choice test (questions are public — you can study the exact pool):

  1. Technician — entry level. Grants VHF/UHF (local, repeaters) plus some HF. Enough for local/regional emergency comms. ~1 evening of study.
  2. General — adds most HF privileges → long-distance / cross-country.
  3. Amateur Extra — full privileges.

Get at least a Technician license now while it's easy, and ideally General so you can use HF for long distance. Learn the emergency rules for your service before you need them, and practice within your license privileges.

The main ham bands and what they do

  • 2 meters (144–148 MHz, VHF) and 70 cm (420–450 MHz, UHF): local simplex + repeaters. Your everyday "talk around town" bands.
  • HF bands (e.g. 80m, 40m, 20m): long distance.
    • 40m (7 MHz): reliable regional (100s of miles), day and night — great for a state/region emergency net.
    • 20m (14 MHz): long-distance (cross-country / international) in daytime.
    • 80m (3.5 MHz): regional at night.

Repeaters — how hams get range from a handheld

A repeater is a radio on a tower/hilltop that receives your weak signal and re-transmits it with a big antenna and power, massively extending range. To use one you set your radio to the repeater's output frequency, an offset (so it transmits on the input), and sometimes a CTCSS "tone." Local repeater lists (and emergency nets) are worth printing out for your area.

Simplex (radio-to-radio, no repeater)

If repeaters are down, you talk simplex — directly, radio to radio, line of sight. The national 2m simplex calling frequency is 146.520 MHz.

Ham digital & data (advanced, FYI)

Hams can also send text/email/images with no internet via Winlink (email over radio), FT8 (weak-signal), and APRS (position/text). Useful but needs more gear and practice.

6. Shortwave (SWL) — hearing the outside world

  • What it is: Using a shortwave receiver to listen to broadcasts on the HF bands (roughly 1.8–30 MHz). No license needed to listen.
  • Why it matters: When local media and internet are down, shortwave lets you hear international news, government/emergency broadcasts, and ham traffic from far away — because HF signals bounce worldwide.
  • What to listen for:
    • International broadcasters (news from outside the affected area).
    • NOAA Weather Radio (VHF 162.400–162.550 MHz) — continuous US weather & emergency alerts. Every emergency kit should have a NOAA weather radio.
    • Ham bands (40m/20m) — to hear emergency nets and relays.
    • AM/FM broadcast and Emergency Alert System (EAS) on a normal radio.
  • Modes: Broadcasts are usually AM; ham/utility voice is often SSB (USB/LSB). A radio with SSB hears far more.
  • Tip: A simple long wire antenna dramatically improves shortwave reception.

7. Antennas, power & range (the practical stuff)

  • Antenna height beats power. Getting the antenna up high (roof, tree, mast) adds more range than buying a bigger amplifier.
  • Use the right antenna for the band. A proper resonant antenna (correct length for the frequency) works far better than a random wire.
  • Line of sight (VHF/UHF): range ≈ limited by the horizon and obstructions. Height = range. Repeaters overcome this.
  • HF skip: range changes with the time of day and solar conditions. Lower bands (80m) are better at night; higher bands (20m) are better in daytime.
  • Power: keep radios charged. Stock spare batteries, a 12V battery, a solar panel, and car-charging cables. A radio with a dead battery is a paperweight.
  • Grounding & safety: never put an antenna where it can touch power lines; ground equipment to reduce static/lightning risk.

8. How to call for help (procedure)

  1. Listen first. Make sure you're not interrupting other emergency traffic.
  2. Use the emergency call:
    • Voice distress: say "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY" (life-threatening) or "PAN-PAN" (urgent but not immediately life-threatening), then your info.
    • On CB, call on Channel 9. On ham, break into the local emergency net or call on a repeater / 146.520 simplex.
  3. Give the essentials — slowly, clearly, repeat:
    • Who you are (name / call sign).
    • Where you are (address, cross-streets, GPS coords, landmarks).
    • What the emergency is (injury, fire, trapped, etc.).
    • What you need (medical, rescue, relay a message).
  4. Spell hard words with the phonetic alphabet (below).
  5. Stay on, don't wander off frequency. Keep the radio on so responders can call back. Repeat your message at intervals if no answer.

NATO phonetic alphabet

Alpha Bravo Charlie Delta Echo Foxtrot Golf Hotel India Juliet Kilo Lima Mike November Oscar Papa Quebec Romeo Sierra Tango Uniform Victor Whiskey X-ray Yankee Zulu. Numbers: say digits individually ("one-two-zero").

Common "pro-words"

  • "Over" = I'm done talking, your turn.
  • "Out" = conversation finished (don't say "over and out").
  • "Roger" / "Copy" = I received and understand.
  • "Say again" = repeat (don't say "repeat").
  • "Affirmative / Negative" = yes / no.
  • "Break" = pause / someone with emergency traffic wants in.

9. A sensible emergency radio kit (shopping / checklist)

  • NOAA weather radio (hand-crank/solar + battery) — alerts & forecasts.
  • Shortwave receiver with SSB — hear news & ham traffic worldwide (RX only).
  • A pair+ of GMRS radios (and a GMRS license) — family/group, best bang for buck. FRS works with no license at shorter range.
  • A dual-band (2m/70cm) ham handheld (HT) — cheap; huge capability with a Technician license. Add a better antenna.
  • (Advanced) An HF ham radio + General license — true long distance.
  • CB if you travel by vehicle/convoy.
  • Power: spare batteries, 12V battery, solar panel, car charger.
  • Better antennas + coax; a roll of wire for an HF/long-wire antenna.
  • Printed frequency card (below), local repeater list, notebook & pencil.
