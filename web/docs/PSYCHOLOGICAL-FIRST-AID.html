Psychological first aid — helping people cope in a crisis

Disasters hurt people's minds as well as their bodies. Over the days and weeks of a crisis, fear, grief, exhaustion and uncertainty are normal reactions to an abnormal situation — not a weakness or an illness. Psychological first aid (PFA) is simple, humane support that any caring person can give. It is not counselling or therapy, and you do not have to be a professional to help.

This is a plain-language guide in our own words, based on the widely taught PFA approach. It is general support, not a substitute for professional mental-health care. For anyone in immediate danger — threatening to harm themselves or others, or unable to care for themselves — get professional help if it is at all reachable.

What PFA is (and is not)

PFA is: staying calm and present, keeping people safe, listening without pressure, meeting basic needs (water, warmth, a toilet, information), and connecting people with loved ones, accurate information and further help.

PFA is not: forcing people to talk about what happened, pushing them to "debrief" or relive details, diagnosing, or giving advice you are not qualified to give. Pressing someone to recount a trauma can make things worse — follow their lead.

Only help if it is safe for you. You cannot help others if you become a casualty.

The three actions: Look, Listen, Link

1. Look

  • Check for safety first — is the scene and the person safe from ongoing danger?
  • Look for anyone with urgent basic needs or serious injury (give physical first aid or route to it first — see the first-aid manuals on this drive).
  • Notice who is in severe distress: frozen and unresponsive, panicking, or unable to function. Calm presence helps most here.

2. Listen

  • Approach gently. Introduce yourself, say you are there to help, and respect their wishes.
  • Help them feel safe and comfortable — a quieter spot, a blanket, water, shade.
  • Listen, don't probe. Let them talk if they want to; let them be silent if they don't. You do not need to fix their feelings or fill every silence. Do not interrupt or rush.
  • Accept how they feel. Don't say "you're lucky" or "I know how you feel." Acknowledge what they've lost. It's enough to say you're sorry this happened and you're here.
  • Help them calm down if they are overwhelmed (see Acute distress below).

3. Link

  • Meet basic needs: water, food, shelter, warmth, a safe place, medical care.
  • Give accurate information when you have it, and say plainly when you don't know. Rumour and uncertainty feed panic; honest, calm updates settle it.
  • Reconnect people with family and friends, and protect those who are alone — especially children, older people, injured people and anyone who can't look after themselves.
  • Connect them to further help — medical, official, community or spiritual support — and help them take the next practical step themselves rather than taking over.

Someone in acute distress

If a person is panicking, hyperventilating, or seems "frozen":

  • Keep your own voice calm, slow and quiet. Stay with them; don't leave them alone.
  • Help them ground: feet flat on the floor, name a few things they can see and hear, slow the breathing (breathe out longer than in). Give them space; don't crowd or grab them.
  • Keep them safe from harm until the surge passes. It usually eases with time and presence.
  • Reconnect them with someone they trust as soon as you can.

Helping children

  • Children take their cues from calm adults. Keep them with trusted caregivers; reunite separated children with family as the top priority, and never leave a child unattended.
  • Keep routines where you can (meals, sleep, simple tasks) — they are reassuring.
  • Answer questions honestly and simply, at their age level. Limit their exposure to frightening scenes and repeated bad news.
  • Expect changes — clinginess, bed-wetting, anger, going quiet. These usually ease with safety, routine and reassurance over time.

Grief and loss

  • Grief is a normal response to loss. There is no single "right" way or timetable.
  • Be present and practical: help with immediate needs, respect the person's culture and customs, and let them grieve their own way. Avoid clichés; listening matters more than words.

Look after yourself and other helpers

Helpers get exhausted, numb or overwhelmed too — this is normal, not failure.

  • Rest, eat and drink in shifts. Take breaks. Swap tasks so no one runs on empty.
  • Talk with other helpers about how the work is affecting you.
  • Notice warning signs in yourself and teammates — can't sleep, constant dread, snapping at people, hopelessness — and ease the load. If distress is severe or lasting, seek help.

When ordinary support isn't enough

Most people recover with safety, time and the support of those around them. Seek professional or more intensive help — if it is reachable — when someone:

  • talks about or plans to harm themselves or others;
  • cannot function at all (not eating, drinking or caring for themselves) after the immediate danger has passed;
  • is lost in time or place, or out of touch with reality;
  • or stays severely distressed for a long time with no improvement.
